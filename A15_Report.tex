\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are a class of deep learning models capable of
synthesising realistic data samples from random noise through an adversarial training
process. This project implements a Deep Convolutional GAN (DCGAN) trained on the MNIST
dataset to generate synthetic handwritten digit images. The Generator learns to map
100-dimensional Gaussian noise vectors, through a linear projection followed by
transposed-convolutional upsampling, into plausible 28$\times$28 greyscale digit images,
while a convolutional Discriminator learns to distinguish real MNIST images from
generated ones. The two networks are trained jointly and adversarially: the Generator
tries to fool the Discriminator, and the Discriminator tries not to be fooled, and this
competition drives both networks to improve over time. Trained over 50 epochs on an
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 laptop GPU, the Generator produces clearly legible and diverse
handwritten digits by the end of training, with a final Generator loss of 3.2384 and a
final Discriminator loss of 0.3445. Generated digit images are visually plausible across
all ten digit classes, and latent space interpolation confirms that the model has learned
a continuous, structured internal representation of handwritten digits rather than
memorising the training data. This report documents the theoretical background, dataset
preparation, model architecture, training procedure, and a detailed analysis of the
results obtained, including an honest discussion of the points at which the training
dynamics departed from the idealised behaviour described in GAN theory.

\section{Problem Statement and Objectives}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Handwritten digit recognition is a well-established classification task in machine
learning, where a model is given an image and must predict which of the ten digit
classes it belongs to. This project addresses the inverse problem: instead of
classifying an existing image, the goal is to generate an entirely new image that looks
like a genuine handwritten digit, starting from nothing but random noise. This is a
significantly harder problem, because there is no single correct answer to check the
output against. The model must instead learn the overall statistical structure of what
handwritten digits look like, in terms of stroke thickness, curvature, slant, and
proportion, and then produce new samples that are consistent with that structure without
copying any specific training example.

This class of problem is known as generative modelling, and it has practical relevance
beyond digits. The same underlying techniques are used to generate synthetic training
data for classifiers when labelled data is scarce, to create realistic images and video
for entertainment and design, to explore the structure of a data distribution for
scientific analysis, and to build denoising and super-resolution systems for real
photographs. This project uses the MNIST dataset of handwritten digits as a
well-understood, low-resolution testbed to study the generative modelling problem before
it is applied to more complex image domains. The specific technique used is a Generative
Adversarial Network (GAN), which frames generative modelling as a competitive game
between two neural networks rather than a single network trying to reconstruct or
approximate the data directly.

\subsection{Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Understand the GAN minimax framework and the adversarial relationship between
          the Generator and the Discriminator, including why training two competing
          networks jointly can produce a useful generative model.
    \item Implement a Deep Convolutional GAN (DCGAN) using PyTorch, with a clearly
          defined Generator and Discriminator, following architectural conventions that
          are known to stabilise GAN training on image data.
    \item Prepare and preprocess the MNIST dataset (60,000 training images of
          handwritten digits, each 28$\times$28 pixels in greyscale) so that it is in a
          form suitable for training the Generator and Discriminator.
    \item Train the DCGAN for 50 epochs on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 laptop GPU,
          recording the Generator loss, Discriminator loss, and Discriminator confidence
          at every epoch.
    \item Generate novel synthetic handwritten digit images from random noise vectors
          that were never seen during training.
    \item Analyse the quality of the generated images through training loss curves,
          direct visual inspection of sample grids at different stages of training, a
          real-versus-generated comparison, and latent space interpolation between two
          random noise vectors.
    \item Save the trained Generator and Discriminator weights so that the model can be
          reloaded and used for inference without retraining.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Literature Review}

\subsection{Generative Adversarial Networks}

Generative Adversarial Networks were introduced by Goodfellow et al.\ \cite{goodfellow2014}
in 2014. The central idea can be understood through an analogy: imagine a forger trying
to produce counterfeit currency, and a detective trying to tell counterfeit notes apart
from genuine ones. At the start, the forger produces obviously fake notes and the
detective catches them easily. Over time, as the detective gets better at spotting fakes,
the forger is forced to improve, and as the forger's fakes get better, the detective is
forced to sharpen its own judgement. If this process continues indefinitely, the forger's
notes eventually become indistinguishable from genuine currency. A GAN implements exactly
this dynamic using two neural networks trained simultaneously:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generator G:} Takes a random noise vector $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$,
          drawn from a simple, fixed distribution such as a standard Gaussian, and maps
          it to a synthetic image $G(\mathbf{z})$. G has no direct access to real training
          images at any point; it only ever sees the feedback signal provided by D.
    \item \textbf{Discriminator D:} Takes an image, real or generated, and outputs a
          single scalar probability $D(x) \in (0, 1)$ representing how likely it believes
          the image is a genuine sample from the training data rather than an output of G.
\end{itemize}

The two networks are trained with opposing objectives, formalised as the following
minimax game:

\begin{equation}
    \min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)]
    + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z} \sim p_z}[\log(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))]
    \label{eq:minimax}
\end{equation}

D is trained to maximise this expression, that is, to assign a high probability to real
images and a low probability to generated images. G is trained to minimise the same
expression from its own side, that is, to produce images that D assigns a high
probability to, effectively trying to push $D(G(\mathbf{z}))$ towards 1. Because G and D
are updated in alternation using gradients from the same loss, an improvement in one
network directly changes the training signal received by the other, which is what makes
GAN training a genuinely adversarial and often unstable process rather than a
straightforward optimisation.

Goodfellow et al.\ show that, for a fixed Generator, the Discriminator that maximises
Equation~\ref{eq:minimax} has a closed form:

\begin{equation}
    D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}
    \label{eq:optimal-d}
\end{equation}

where $p_{\text{data}}$ is the true data distribution and $p_g$ is the distribution
implicitly defined by the Generator. At the theoretical point of convergence, known as
the Nash equilibrium of the game, the Generator's distribution exactly matches the data
distribution, so that $p_{\text{data}}(x) = p_g(x)$ for every $x$. Substituting this into
Equation~\ref{eq:optimal-d} gives $D^*(x) = 0.5$ everywhere: the Discriminator can no
longer tell real and generated images apart and is reduced to guessing. This theoretical
target value of 0.5 is used later in this report (Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}) as a
reference point when interpreting the Discriminator's output during training.

\subsection{Loss Function}

Both networks in this project are trained using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss, which is
the standard loss function for binary classification problems:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} = -\left[ y \log \hat{y} + (1 - y) \log(1 - \hat{y}) \right]
    \label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}

Here $y \in \{0, 1\}$ is the ground-truth label, where $y=1$ denotes a real image and
$y=0$ denotes a generated image, and $\hat{y} = D(x)$ is the Discriminator's predicted
probability that the input is real. When $y=1$, the loss reduces to $-\log \hat{y}$,
which is small when the Discriminator confidently predicts "real" and grows sharply as
its prediction approaches 0. When $y=0$, the loss reduces to $-\log(1-\hat{y})$, which
behaves symmetrically. The Discriminator's total loss on a batch is the average BCE loss
computed once with real images labelled 1 and once with generated images labelled 0, and
the two are summed. The Generator's loss is computed by passing its generated images
through the Discriminator again, this time labelling them as 1, so that minimising BCE
loss directly corresponds to the Generator trying to make the Discriminator output values
close to 1 for its fakes. This use of BCE loss is what makes Equation~\ref{eq:minimax}
practical to optimise with standard gradient-based training.

\subsection{MNIST Dataset}

The MNIST dataset \cite{lecun1998} contains 70,000 greyscale images of handwritten digits
(digits 0 through 9), each 28$\times$28 pixels in size, compiled by LeCun, Cortes, and
Burges from earlier NIST handwriting samples. It is split into 60,000 training images and
10,000 test images. Because the images are small, single-channel, and centred, and
because the dataset is large enough to train a generative model without overfitting, MNIST
has become the standard first benchmark for new generative modelling techniques,
including GANs, VAEs, and diffusion models, before those techniques are scaled up to more
complex, higher-resolution image datasets. This project uses only the 60,000-image
training split, since digit labels are not required for GAN training and no held-out
test evaluation is performed on classification accuracy.

\subsection{GAN versus Variational Autoencoder}

GANs are one of two dominant approaches to generative modelling of images, the other
being the Variational Autoencoder (VAE) \cite{kingma2013}. A VAE learns an encoder that
compresses an image into a latent vector and a decoder that reconstructs the image from
that vector, with an additional term in its loss that regularises the latent space to
follow a known distribution. Where a GAN's Generator receives no direct pixel-level
supervision and is instead guided entirely by whether it fools the Discriminator, a VAE's
decoder is trained with a direct reconstruction loss against real images, which tends to
produce smoother but blurrier outputs. Table~\ref{tab:ganvsvae} summarises the key
differences between the two approaches.

\begin{table}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}
            \hline
            \textbf{Property} & \textbf{GAN} & \textbf{VAE} \\
            \hline
            Training objective & Adversarial minimax & ELBO (reconstruction + KL) \\
            \hline
            Output quality & Sharp, realistic images & Tends to be blurry \\
            \hline
            Training stability & Can be unstable & Generally stable \\
            \hline
            Latent space & Unstructured noise & Explicitly regularised ($\mathcal{N}(0,I)$) \\
            \hline
            Mode collapse risk & Yes & No \\
            \hline
            Evaluation & Qualitative / FID & Reconstruction loss / ELBO \\
            \hline
        \end{tabular}
        \caption{Comparison of GAN and VAE for image generation}
        \label{tab:ganvsvae}
    \end{center}
\end{table}

GANs were chosen for this project because they are known to produce sharper, more
visually realistic images than VAEs at comparable model sizes, which directly serves the
goal of generating convincing handwritten digits. The trade-off, discussed in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, is that GAN training is less stable and does not always
settle into the balanced equilibrium that the theory predicts.

\subsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}

The raw MNIST images cannot be fed directly into the Generator and Discriminator; they
must first be converted into a numerical format that is compatible with the networks and
scaled to a range that matches the Generator's output activation. This was done as a
short, fixed pipeline of four steps, applied identically to every image before it is
used in training.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Load the raw image.} \texttt{torchvision.datasets.MNIST} downloads the
          dataset automatically on first use and loads each image as a
          28$\times$28 greyscale PIL image with pixel values in the range $[0, 255]$.
    \item \textbf{Convert to a tensor.} The \texttt{ToTensor()} transform converts the
          PIL image into a PyTorch tensor and simultaneously rescales pixel values from
          the integer range $[0, 255]$ to the floating-point range $[0, 1]$, which is the
          format PyTorch models expect.
    \item \textbf{Normalise to $[-1, 1]$.} The \texttt{Normalize([0.5], [0.5])} transform
          applies $x' = (x - 0.5) / 0.5$ to every pixel, remapping the range from
          $[0, 1]$ to $[-1, 1]$. This step exists specifically because the Generator's
          final layer uses a Tanh activation, which also produces outputs in
          $[-1, 1]$. If this step were skipped, the Discriminator would immediately be
          able to tell real and generated images apart purely by their different pixel
          ranges, regardless of digit content, which would prevent meaningful adversarial
          training from taking place.
    \item \textbf{Batch and shuffle.} The preprocessed images are grouped into batches of
          128 using a \texttt{DataLoader}, with shuffling enabled so that every epoch
          sees the images in a different order. Shuffling prevents the Discriminator from
          learning spurious patterns tied to the fixed ordering of the dataset, and a
          batch size of 128 was chosen as a balance between providing the Discriminator
          with a sufficiently diverse mix of images per update and keeping GPU memory
          usage low enough to train comfortably on a laptop GPU.
\end{enumerate}

A sample of real MNIST images after this preprocessing pipeline, shown after
denormalisation for display, is given in Figure~\ref{fig:realsamples}.

\section{Methodology and Architecture}

This section describes how the Generator and Discriminator are structured internally,
and the step-by-step procedure used to train them together. The overall training
methodology can be summarised as a repeating five-step cycle, executed once per batch of
images:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sample a batch of random noise vectors from a standard Gaussian distribution.
    \item Pass the noise through the Generator to produce a batch of fake images.
    \item Pass a batch of real images and the batch of fake images through the
          Discriminator separately, and compute how well the Discriminator distinguishes
          them.
    \item Update the Discriminator's weights to improve this distinction.
    \item Pass the same fake images through the updated Discriminator a second time, and
          update the Generator's weights so that the Discriminator is more likely to
          classify them as real.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this section details the internal structure of the two networks that
this cycle operates on, followed by a precise, code-level description of the training
procedure itself.

\subsection{Generator}

The Generator follows the Deep Convolutional GAN (DCGAN) design proposed by
Radford et al.\ \cite{radford2015}. Rather than directly producing a 784-pixel flat
vector, it first expands the 100-dimensional noise vector into a small spatial feature
map and then progressively upsamples that feature map to the full image resolution using
transposed convolutions, which are learned upsampling operations rather than fixed
interpolation. This design allows the network to build up an image from coarse
structure to fine detail, in a manner broadly analogous to how convolutional classifiers
extract features from fine detail to coarse structure in the opposite direction.

\begin{table}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tabular}{|c|l|c|}
            \hline
            \textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Operation} & \textbf{Output Shape} \\
            \hline
            Input & Noise vector $\mathbf{z}$ & $(100,)$ \\
            \hline
            1 & Linear$(100 \to 256 \times 7 \times 7)$ + BatchNorm1d + ReLU & $(12544,)$ \\
            \hline
            Reshape & \texttt{view(-1, 256, 7, 7)} & $(256, 7, 7)$ \\
            \hline
            2 & ConvTranspose2d$(256 \to 128,\, k{=}4, s{=}2, p{=}1)$ + BatchNorm2d + ReLU & $(128, 14, 14)$ \\
            \hline
            3 & ConvTranspose2d$(128 \to 64,\, k{=}4, s{=}2, p{=}1)$ + BatchNorm2d + ReLU & $(64, 28, 28)$ \\
            \hline
            4 & Conv2d$(64 \to 1,\, k{=}3, s{=}1, p{=}1)$ + Tanh & $(1, 28, 28)$ \\
            \hline
        \end{tabular}
        \caption{Generator architecture}
        \label{tab:generator}
    \end{center}
\end{table}

Reading Table~\ref{tab:generator} as a sequence of steps: the 100-dimensional noise
vector is first projected by a fully connected layer into 12,544 values, which is exactly
$256 \times 7 \times 7$, and reshaped into a $256$-channel, $7 \times 7$ feature map. This
feature map carries no spatial meaning yet; it is simply a convenient starting point for
the convolutional layers that follow. The first ConvTranspose2d layer doubles the spatial
resolution from $7 \times 7$ to $14 \times 14$ while reducing the channel depth from 256
to 128. The second ConvTranspose2d layer again doubles the resolution, from $14 \times 14$
to $28 \times 28$, reducing the channel depth from 128 to 64, which is the target image
resolution. A final ordinary Conv2d layer collapses the 64 channels down to a single
greyscale channel. BatchNorm is applied after every layer except the very last, which
stabilises training by keeping the distribution of activations consistent as the weights
change during training. Tanh is used only at the final output so that pixel values fall
in $[-1, 1]$, matching the normalised range of the real training images described in
Section~3.1.

\subsection{Discriminator}

The Discriminator performs the reverse operation to the Generator, taking a $28 \times 28$
image and reducing it step by step to a single probability, using strided convolutions
instead of pooling layers to perform the downsampling. This follows the DCGAN convention
that pooling operations, which are fixed and non-learnable, should be replaced with
learned strided convolutions wherever possible.

\begin{table}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tabular}{|c|l|c|}
            \hline
            \textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Operation} & \textbf{Output Shape} \\
            \hline
            Input & Image & $(1, 28, 28)$ \\
            \hline
            1 & Conv2d$(1 \to 64,\, k{=}4, s{=}2, p{=}1)$ + LeakyReLU(0.2) & $(64, 14, 14)$ \\
            \hline
            2 & Conv2d$(64 \to 128,\, k{=}4, s{=}2, p{=}1)$ + BatchNorm2d + LeakyReLU(0.2) & $(128, 7, 7)$ \\
            \hline
            3 & Conv2d$(128 \to 256,\, k{=}4, s{=}2, p{=}1)$ + BatchNorm2d + LeakyReLU(0.2) & $(256, 3, 3)$ \\
            \hline
            Flatten & \texttt{Flatten()} & $(2304,)$ \\
            \hline
            4 & Linear$(2304 \to 1)$ + Sigmoid & $(1,)$ \\
            \hline
        \end{tabular}
        \caption{Discriminator architecture}
        \label{tab:discriminator}
    \end{center}
\end{table}

Following Table~\ref{tab:discriminator} step by step: the first convolutional layer
halves the spatial resolution from $28 \times 28$ to $14 \times 14$ while increasing the
channel depth from 1 to 64, extracting low-level features such as edges and strokes.
The second layer halves the resolution again to $7 \times 7$ with 128 channels, and the
third reduces it to $3 \times 3$ with 256 channels, by which point the representation has
moved from raw pixels to a compact, high-level summary of the image. This is then
flattened into a single vector of length 2304 and passed through one final linear layer
that reduces it to a single value, which the Sigmoid activation squashes into the range
$(0, 1)$ and which is interpreted as the probability that the input image is real.
BatchNorm is deliberately omitted from the first convolutional layer, following DCGAN
convention, so that the network sees the true, unnormalised distribution of the raw
input at least once. LeakyReLU(0.2) is used instead of standard ReLU in every hidden
layer of the Discriminator because standard ReLU can cause a neuron to become
permanently inactive once it receives a negative input, a problem sometimes called a
dead neuron; LeakyReLU avoids this by passing a small fraction of negative inputs through
unchanged, computed as $f(x) = \max(0.2x, x)$.

\subsection{Weight Initialisation}

Both networks use the DCGAN weight initialisation scheme: the weights of every
convolutional and linear layer are drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0 and
standard deviation 0.02, and the weights of every BatchNorm layer are drawn from a normal
distribution with mean 1 and standard deviation 0.02, with BatchNorm biases set to 0.
This specific initialisation was found empirically by Radford et al.\ \cite{radford2015}
to make DCGAN training noticeably more stable than the default initialisation schemes
built into most deep learning frameworks, and it is applied to both networks before
training begins.

\subsection{Training Procedure}

Each training batch performs two alternating updates, described below in the same order
in which they occur in the implementation.

\textbf{Step A, train the Discriminator:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Take a batch of real images from the training set and feed them through D,
          with the target label set to 1 for every image, since they are genuinely real.
          Compute the resulting loss, $\mathcal{L}_{D,\text{real}}$, using
          Equation~\ref{eq:bce}.
    \item Sample a fresh batch of random noise vectors and generate a batch of fake
          images by feeding the noise through G. Feed these fake images through D, with
          the target label set to 0 for every image. Compute the resulting loss,
          $\mathcal{L}_{D,\text{fake}}$.
    \item Add the two losses together to get the total Discriminator loss,
          $\mathcal{L}_D = \mathcal{L}_{D,\text{real}} + \mathcal{L}_{D,\text{fake}}$.
          Backpropagate this loss and update only the Discriminator's weights.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Step B, train the Generator:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Feed the same batch of fake images through the Discriminator a second time,
          but this time with the target label set to 1, reflecting what the Generator
          wants the Discriminator to believe.
    \item Compute the resulting loss, $\mathcal{L}_G$, and backpropagate it through the
          Discriminator and into the Generator. Only the Generator's weights are updated
          in this step; the Discriminator's weights are left unchanged even though the
          gradient passes through it.
\end{enumerate}

A crucial implementation detail is the \texttt{.detach()} call applied to the fake images
during Step A. This tells PyTorch to treat the fake images as fixed constants for the
purpose of computing $\mathcal{L}_{D,\text{fake}}$, which prevents gradients from that
step from flowing back into the Generator. Without this call, updating the Discriminator
would also nudge the Generator's weights in an unintended direction at the wrong point in
the cycle. Separate Adam optimisers are maintained for G and D, so that the two update
steps described above never interfere with each other's internal optimiser state, such as
momentum estimates.

\section{Implementation}

This section describes how the methodology in Section~4 was translated into a working
PyTorch program, following the exact sequence of steps executed by \texttt{Source\_code.py}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fix the random seed and select the device.} The seeds for Python's
          \texttt{random} module, NumPy, and PyTorch are all set to 42 at the start of
          the script, so that dataset shuffling, weight initialisation, and noise
          sampling are reproducible across runs. The script then checks for a CUDA-capable
          GPU and uses it if available, falling back to the CPU otherwise.
    \item \textbf{Define hyperparameters.} All of the constants used throughout training,
          including the latent dimension, batch size, number of epochs, and learning
          rate, are defined together at the top of the script, listed in
          Table~\ref{tab:setup}.
    \item \textbf{Load and preprocess the dataset.} The MNIST training split is loaded
          through \texttt{torchvision.datasets.MNIST} and passed through the
          preprocessing pipeline described in Section~3.1, then wrapped in a
          \texttt{DataLoader} that yields shuffled batches of 128 images.
    \item \textbf{Build the Generator and Discriminator.} Both networks are instantiated
          from the classes described in Sections~4.1 and 4.2, moved to the selected
          device, and initialised using the scheme described in Section~4.3.
    \item \textbf{Define the loss function and optimisers.} A single
          \texttt{nn.BCELoss} instance is shared by both networks, and two independent
          Adam optimisers are created, one for G and one for D, both using a learning
          rate of 0.0002 and betas of (0.5, 0.999). A fixed batch of 64 noise vectors is
          also created at this point and reused at every logging step, so that the same
          64 noise vectors can be tracked visually across the whole of training.
    \item \textbf{Run the training loop.} For each of the 50 epochs, every batch in the
          training set is processed once using the two-step update procedure described in
          Section~4.4. The average Generator loss, Discriminator loss, and Discriminator
          confidence on real and fake images are accumulated across all batches in the
          epoch and printed to the console at the end of the epoch.
    \item \textbf{Save sample grids periodically.} After epoch 1 and after every 5th
          epoch thereafter, the fixed noise batch is passed through the current Generator
          in evaluation mode, and the resulting 64 images are arranged into an
          8$\times$8 grid and saved to \texttt{outputs/samples/}, which allows the
          progression of image quality to be inspected after training without needing to
          rerun the model.
    \item \textbf{Generate post-training analysis outputs.} Once all 50 epochs are
          complete, the script produces the loss and confidence curves, a side-by-side
          comparison of real and generated images, a latent space interpolation between
          two random noise vectors, and a final grid of 100 newly generated digits.
    \item \textbf{Save the trained model.} The final weights of both the Generator and
          the Discriminator are saved to \texttt{outputs/models/} using
          \texttt{torch.save(model.state\_dict(), path)}, which stores only the learned
          parameters and allows the model to be reloaded later for inference without
          needing to repeat training.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hyperparameter Justification}

The choice of hyperparameters follows established GAN training practices reported in the
literature summarised in Table~\ref{tab:literature}, rather than being chosen arbitrarily.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learning rate = 0.0002:} This value is recommended by
          Radford et al.\ \cite{radford2015} specifically for GAN training. A higher
          learning rate tends to cause the Generator and Discriminator to overshoot each
          other's optimal response and oscillate, while a much lower learning rate slows
          convergence without providing any additional stability benefit in practice.
    \item \textbf{Adam optimiser ($\beta_1 = 0.5$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$):} Adam adapts the
          effective learning rate for each individual parameter based on estimates of its
          recent gradient history, which generally stabilises training compared to plain
          stochastic gradient descent. The reduced value of $\beta_1 = 0.5$, in place of
          the more common default of 0.9, is a DCGAN-specific recommendation
          \cite{radford2015} that reduces the amount of momentum carried between updates,
          which in turn dampens some of the oscillation that is characteristic of
          adversarial training.
    \item \textbf{Latent dimension = 100:} A 100-dimensional noise vector gives the
          Generator enough room to encode the variation seen across the ten digit
          classes, including differences in stroke width, slant, and shape, without the
          latent space becoming so large that most of it is rarely sampled during
          training.
    \item \textbf{Batch size = 128:} A batch of this size gives the Discriminator a
          reasonably diverse mix of real and fake images on every update, which produces
          more stable gradient estimates than very small batches, while remaining well
          within the memory limits of a laptop GPU.
    \item \textbf{Epochs = 50:} This was sufficient for the Generator to produce
          consistently legible and diverse digits, as discussed in
          Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, even though the adversarial losses continued to
          move apart rather than converge over this range, without spending more compute
          than necessary for a mini project of this scope.
\end{itemize}

The core of the training loop, corresponding to Step A and Step B in
Section~4.4, is reproduced below.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Core training loop]
# LATENT_DIM = 100
for real_imgs, _ in train_loader:
    real_imgs = real_imgs.to(DEVICE)
    bsz = real_imgs.size(0)

    # Step A: train the Discriminator
    D.zero_grad()
    real_labels = torch.ones(bsz, 1, device=DEVICE)
    fake_labels = torch.zeros(bsz, 1, device=DEVICE)
    loss_d_real = criterion(D(real_imgs), real_labels)

    z = torch.randn(bsz, LATENT_DIM, device=DEVICE)
    fake_imgs = G(z)
    loss_d_fake = criterion(D(fake_imgs.detach()), fake_labels)

    loss_D = loss_d_real + loss_d_fake
    loss_D.backward()
    opt_D.step()

    # Step B: train the Generator
    G.zero_grad()
    loss_G = criterion(D(fake_imgs), real_labels)
    loss_G.backward()
    opt_G.step()
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Analysis of Training Dynamics}
\label{sec:dynamics}

In the earliest epochs, the Generator loss starts at 2.0551, while the Discriminator
loss is comparatively low at 0.6038, and the Discriminator's average confidence is
already $D(x) \approx 0.78$ on real images against $D(G(z)) \approx 0.23$ on fake images.
This means the Discriminator is separating real from fake fairly confidently within the
very first epoch. This happens sooner than it would for a plain fully-connected
Generator starting from unstructured noise, because the convolutional upsampling path
used here produces coarse but already somewhat structured digit shapes from the very
first forward pass, giving the Discriminator an early foothold.

From epoch 1 through epoch 50, the Generator loss rises steadily and almost
monotonically, from 2.0551 to 3.2384, while the Discriminator loss falls over the same
range, from 0.6038 to 0.3445. The corresponding confidence values move in the same
direction: $D(x)$ climbs from 0.778 to 0.867, and $D(G(z))$ falls from 0.230 to 0.133.
This is the opposite of the textbook convergence behaviour described in Section~2.1,
where both quantities would approach 0.5 and the Discriminator loss would approach
$-2\log(0.5) = \log 2 \approx 0.693$ as the system reaches Nash equilibrium. In this
run, the Discriminator steadily gained the upper hand over the Generator across the full
50 epochs, rather than the two networks settling into a balanced standoff.

This Discriminator-dominant pattern is a well-documented and fairly common outcome when
training a DCGAN on MNIST, and it does not by itself indicate that the Generator failed
to learn anything useful. Salimans et al.\ \cite{salimans2016} note that raw BCE loss
values and Discriminator confidence scores correlate poorly with the actual perceptual
quality of generated images. In line with that observation, the generated digit grids
shown in Section~\ref{sec:visualquality} become progressively sharper, more legible, and
more diverse as training proceeds, even as the Discriminator's confidence keeps rising.
This indicates that the Generator continued to make genuine visual progress in practice,
despite steadily losing ground in the adversarial loss. A longer training run, a reduced
learning rate specifically for the Discriminator, or one-sided label smoothing on the
real labels are standard techniques that could be used to rebalance this dynamic further,
and are discussed as future work in Section~\ref{sec:future}.

\subsection{Mode Collapse Analysis}
\label{sec:modecollapse}

Mode collapse is a well-known failure mode in GAN training in which the Generator
learns to produce only a narrow subset of possible outputs, for example generating only
one or two digit shapes regardless of the input noise vector \cite{salimans2016}. It
typically manifests during training as a sudden drop in Generator loss combined with a
plateau in Discriminator loss, since the Discriminator can no longer usefully
distinguish between a small number of repeated, collapsed outputs.

In this experiment, mode collapse was not observed. The evidence supporting this
conclusion is as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The 100-sample grid in Figure~\ref{fig:final100} shows clear diversity across
          multiple digit classes, with visually distinct stroke styles, thicknesses, and
          orientations even within samples of the same digit.
    \item The latent interpolation in Figure~\ref{fig:interp} produces smooth, varied
          transitions between different digit shapes as the interpolation coefficient
          changes, confirming that distinct regions of the 100-dimensional latent space
          map to distinct, meaningful outputs rather than all collapsing to the same
          image.
    \item The Generator loss increases gradually and almost monotonically across all 50
          epochs, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:losses}, with no sudden drop or plateau of
          the kind associated with collapse visible anywhere in the recorded curves.
\end{enumerate}

The convolutional DCGAN architecture used here, combined with BatchNorm, LeakyReLU
activations, and a moderately sized 100-dimensional latent space, follows the specific
design recommendations of Radford et al.\ \cite{radford2015} that were found to reduce
the tendency of the Generator to settle on degenerate, low-diversity solutions early in
training.

To verify that the Generator has learned a structured internal representation rather
than simply memorising individual training examples, linear interpolation was performed
between two randomly sampled noise vectors, $\mathbf{z}_1$ and $\mathbf{z}_2$, using ten
evenly spaced interpolation coefficients:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_\alpha = (1 - \alpha)\,\mathbf{z}_1 + \alpha\,\mathbf{z}_2, \quad \alpha \in [0, 1]
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{outputs/samples/latent_interpolation.png}
    \caption{Latent space interpolation from $\mathbf{z}_1$ to $\mathbf{z}_2$ in 10 steps}

If the Generator had simply memorised specific training images, interpolating between
two unrelated noise vectors would be expected to produce an abrupt, discontinuous jump
somewhere along the sequence, or a blurred average with no digit-like structure at all.
Instead, the sequence in Figure~\ref{fig:interp} shows a smooth, continuous
transformation from one digit shape into another, with every intermediate image still
resembling a plausible handwritten digit rather than an incoherent blend. This confirms
that the 100-dimensional latent space learned by the Generator is continuous and
semantically organised, in the sense that nearby points in that space correspond to
visually similar digits.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item No quantitative image-quality metric, such as the Fr\'{e}chet Inception
          Distance (FID), was computed for this project, owing to the additional compute
          and the need for a separate pretrained feature-extraction network that this
          would require. Evaluation in this report is therefore qualitative, based on
          visual inspection and the training loss curves.
    \item As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, the Discriminator steadily
          out-paced the Generator in adversarial loss across the full 50 epochs, rather
          than settling at the theoretical Nash equilibrium described in
          Section~2.1. Longer training, a reduced Discriminator learning rate, or
          one-sided label smoothing are standard techniques that could improve this
          balance in a follow-up run.
    \item The model generates digits without any control over which digit class is
          produced; each noise vector maps to whichever digit the Generator has learned
          to associate with that region of the latent space. A Conditional GAN would be
          required to allow a specific digit to be requested on demand.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Scope}

\subsection{Conclusion}

A Deep Convolutional GAN was implemented from first principles in PyTorch and trained on
the MNIST dataset over 50 epochs, successfully learning to generate realistic synthetic
handwritten digit images from 100-dimensional Gaussian noise. The theoretical background
of the GAN minimax game, the practical DCGAN architecture and training conventions that
make that game tractable to optimise, and the resulting training behaviour were all
documented and cross-checked against one another in this report. While the adversarial
loss did not settle at the theoretical Nash equilibrium, with the Discriminator growing
steadily more confident over the course of training and finishing with a Generator loss
of 3.2384 against a Discriminator loss of 0.3445, the generated digits are nonetheless
visually plausible and diverse across all ten digit classes by the end of training. No
evidence of mode collapse was found in either the loss curves or the generated samples.
Latent space interpolation confirmed that the Generator has learned a smooth, continuous
internal representation of handwritten digits, rather than memorising specific training
examples. Taken together, these results validate the core GAN principle described in
Section~2.1: two competing networks, trained jointly on nothing more than a shared
adversarial loss, are capable of producing a working generative model, even in a run
where the adversarial balance clearly favours one network over the other.

\subsection{Future Scope}
\label{sec:future}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Conditional GAN (cGAN):} Extend the Generator and Discriminator to
          condition on the digit class label, following Mirza and
          Osindero \cite{mirza2014}, so that a specific digit can be requested rather
          than obtained by chance from a random noise vector.
    \item \textbf{Wasserstein GAN (WGAN):} Replace the BCE loss used in this project with
          the Wasserstein distance proposed by Arjovsky et al.\ \cite{arjovsky2017}, which
          is reported to give more stable training and a loss value that correlates more
          closely with sample quality than the BCE-based losses examined in
          Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}.
    \item \textbf{Discriminator regularisation:} Apply one-sided label smoothing on the
          real labels, or reduce the Discriminator's learning rate relative to the
          Generator's, to directly counter the Discriminator-dominant dynamic observed in
          Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} and move the training closer to the theoretical
          equilibrium.
    \item \textbf{Quantitative evaluation with FID:} Compute the Fr\'{e}chet Inception
          Distance between the real and generated image distributions, to complement the
          qualitative visual analysis in this report with a single, comparable
          quantitative score.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic data augmentation:} Use the trained Generator to produce
          additional training samples for a downstream digit classifier, particularly for
          any digit classes that are underrepresented in a given training set.
\end{enumerate}
